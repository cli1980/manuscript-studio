The rain wove the night into a web, and the dim streetlights cast one circle of light after another on the sidewalk. Raindrops tapped against the tin of an abandoned advertisement, hollow echoes rippling through the deserted street. The rainwater gathered into small streams, carrying cigarette butts and fallen leaves into the sewer grates.

Connor had just left the company when the last light in the building went out. The research building stood silently in the rainy night like an empty shell drained of its soul. All the windows had become dark, hollow eye sockets, and winding rain streaks crawled up the walls.

It wasn’t until the guard closed the building’s door that he realized he had forgotten his umbrella. Under the eaves, he reached out a hand to test the rain—it wasn’t heavy, and he didn’t want to go back into that pitch-black building to get an umbrella.

Everything around him was soaked in the thick night. After a few seconds of mental preparation, he stepped out from under the eaves and into a shallow puddle. The cold rain pricked the back of his neck like fine needles; his tie whipped over his shoulder as he ran, soaked with rain and turning into an icy snake, tightly constricting his neck.

He had never left the company this late before, but he knew where the bus stop was. All he could do was run toward his destination before the rain got worse.

He saw the bus stop sign’s fluorescent light illuminating the broken reflections in the puddles. He rushed over, stumbling as he lunged for the bench under the shelter.

Connor glanced at his rain-soaked watch, put it in his pocket, then took off his glasses and wiped the raindrops from the lenses with his clothes. He panted amid the ticking of the second hand and the sound of raindrops hitting the slightly rusted metal shelter, staring at the neon sign of the convenience store across the street, which had gone dark.

His phone rang—it was his colleague, Hayes.

“Hello?”

“Langford? It’s me, Hayes.”

“Hayes? Why are you calling me so late?”

“Langford, I’m leaving. I’m transferring to another department.”

“Leaving? Where to?” Connor asked.

“I don’t know either.”

“You don’t know? Then what are you going there for? Is the pay better?”

“I really don’t know anything. They didn’t tell me, and I have to leave soon—tonight.”

“Tonight? So suddenly.” Connor was puzzled.

“Yeah... and you might not be able to reach me anymore.”

“Are you going to work for some secret organization? Come on, you’re so capable, you’ll do well wherever you go.”

“You sound tired. Get some rest early.”

“I wish I could just rest now,” Connor thought.

The moment the call ended, Connor wished Hayes had said more. At least on such a bleak night, hearing another person’s voice was a kind of warmth.

Occasionally, car headlights swept past in the distance. Time and again, he instinctively leaned forward, only to see it was just a passing sedan. He sat wearily on the bench, too tired to take out his watch again, measuring time only by the changing intensity of the rain.

Suddenly, he realized he might have missed the last bus—he had never left the company this late before, so he didn’t know. But the rain kept falling, so all he could do was keep sitting, becoming part of the bus stop.

“Hello?”

The voice seemed to come from the horizon—clear, but unreal.

He suddenly looked up. The bus stop light glowed behind the figure, outlining a vague silhouette—like a watercolor diluted by rain. She held a transparent umbrella, with tiny droplets hanging from its ribs.

When had she arrived?

“Hello...” Connor answered tiredly.

Silence.

After a while, Connor turned back and asked, “Will this bus... still come?”

She nodded, “The bus will come.”

Silence.

“Have you never taken the bus this late before?” she asked.

“Never,” he replied. “I was held up today—and it just happened to rain.”

“I get off work this late every day, always alone. It’s rare to meet someone else this late...” She turned her head. “It’s nice to meet you.”

Connor turned his head and noticed a faint white mark at the base of her finger. His observation skills had never been so detective-like, but he just happened to notice.

“Do you work in a lab?”

“Yes, I often do... biochemical experiments.”

“What a coincidence, so do I.”

“You do?”

“Mm.”

Silence.

“My name’s Langford. I work at a nearby research institute.”

“Hello, Langford. I’m Reed,” she replied. “Serena Reed.”

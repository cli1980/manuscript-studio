I braved the severe cold and returned to my hometown, which is more than two thousand miles away and from which I had been separated for over twenty years.

Since it was deep winter, as I neared my hometown, the weather turned gloomy again. The cold wind blew into the cabin, whistling. Looking out through the gaps in the canopy, under the pale yellow sky, there were several desolate and bleak villages scattered near and far, without a trace of life. My heart couldn't help but feel sorrowful. Ah! Is this really the hometown I have remembered all these years?

The hometown I remember was nothing like this. My hometown was much better. But when I try to recall its beauty and speak of its charms, there are no images or words that come to mind. It seems it was always like this. So I explained to myself: the hometown was originally like this—although it had not progressed, it might not have been as sorrowful as I felt; this was just a change in my own mood, because I had no good feelings about returning this time.

This time, I came specifically to say goodbye. The old house where our extended family had lived together for many years had already been sold to another family. The deadline for handing over the house was this year, so I had to leave before the first day of the lunar new year, to bid a final farewell to the familiar old house and, far from the familiar hometown, move to a foreign place where I sought my livelihood.

Early the next morning, I arrived at my family's doorstep. Many dry grass stalks on the roof tiles were shaking in the wind, indicating the reason why this old house was inevitably changing hands. Several relatives had probably already moved out, so it was very quiet. I arrived outside my own house, and my mother had already come out to greet me, followed by my eight-year-old nephew, Hong'er, who ran out excitedly.

My mother was very happy but also hid much sadness in her expression. She told me to sit down, rest, drink tea, and not talk about moving. Hong'er had never seen me before and stood at a distance, just watching.

But eventually, we talked about moving. I said that the place outside had already been rented, and I had bought a few pieces of furniture. Besides, all the wooden furniture at home had to be sold to buy more. My mother agreed, and the luggage was roughly gathered. The wooden furniture, inconvenient to move, had been half sold, but the money had not yet been collected.

"Rest for a day or two, visit relatives and family once, then we can leave," my mother said.

"Yes."

"And Runtu, whenever he comes to our house, always asks about you and really wants to see you once. I have already told him roughly when you would arrive home; he might come soon."

At that moment, a strange image suddenly flashed through my mind: a golden full moon hung in the deep blue sky. Below was the sandy beach by the sea, planted with endless green watermelons. Among them was a boy of about eleven or twelve years old, wearing a silver ring around his neck, holding a steel fork in his hand, trying hard to stab a raccoon dog. But the raccoon dog twisted its body and escaped from under his crotch.

Connor withdrew his thoughts and put the GOC employment letter into the pocket of his trench coat. He regretted choosing a rainy day to go out, regretted not having a Foundation-funded dinner before his Level 2 clearance expired, and regretted not giving Serena a kiss before leaving.

Connor forgot his umbrella again. He pulled up the collar of his trench coat and walked into the drizzle, glanced back once, then resolutely moved forward.

That day was just an ordinary day for the Foundation. But for the Langford couple, that day was enough to change their entire lives.

Many amino acids connect one by one like train cars to form a polypeptide chain. The structure formed by the folding and twisting of the polypeptide chain is called a protein. Proteins are the bricks and cornerstone of the palace of life; most of the human body's limbs and structures are made of proteins, and many enzymes are also proteins. Humans have discovered tens of thousands of proteins, all composed of only 21 kinds of amino acids arranged in different structures.

Due to the complexity of protein composition and structure, it is currently impossible to synthesize proteins with large molecular weights. Some say that once humans master proteins, they will master life itself.

—Excerpt from "SCPF Basic Biology Tutorial," page 32

When he stepped through the doors of the GOC laboratory building, the weight of the mission pressed down on Connor. Unlike the calmness of Foundation facilities, this maze-like building was filled with quasi-military discipline—the fluorescent tubes emitted a sickly yellow light, rather than the blue glow of Site-18.

"Ah—welcome," the voice was like sticky syrup, "It's really delightful to have a new friend again."

The speaker was Whitmore, Connor’s new superior, a pale white man in his forties whose wrinkled three-piece suit hung on him like a discarded snake skin. The eternal half-smile at the corner of his mouth seemed to suggest he had just remembered a joke at your expense.

He led Connor into a laboratory. Flickering bulbs cast shadows that gathered among the cluttered reagent shelves; the only tidy spot was a stainless steel workbench.

"Most researchers come here for our protein synthesis technology," Whitmore said with a hint of a mocking smile, "Ironically, that also helps us recruit top scientists... for other projects." He snapped his fingers. "Before you get access to the protein tech, you have to prove you’re good enough—Carter, come take the newbie."

A researcher named Carter approached and extended his hand, shaking Connor’s warmly.

Whitmore deliberately adjusted his wrinkled tie slowly. "Relax, work hard. If anything happens... an experimental accident," his laughter carried warmth, "we provide global corpse shipping—for your family’s funeral, prepaid! Ha!" His smile vanished instantly. "And metal detectors ensure no unauthorized 'souvenirs' leave this facility."

He stopped at the door, turning back to toss out a line: "Unlike those idiot organizations that hand out memory wipes like candy, we prefer poison. One dose, permanent effect."

* * *

The first project Connor joined had nothing to do with protein synthesis—he was responsible for improving the interlayer material of agent protective suits.

The first time he saw the sample, Connor was stunned—it was a deep black, like the eye of the cosmic abyss staring fixedly at him, a thickness so dense even starlight would drown in it, like thousands of overlapping raven feathers. He reached out to touch it; smooth, yet resilient, like a shell.

"What is it made of?" Connor asked.

"A special kind of plastic we call 'Black Swan,' reverse-engineered from the corpse of a supernatural creature," Carter replied. "Don’t worry, that thing’s long dead."

"Why have a biologist do materials science work?"

"This stuff resists strong acids and bases, but we don’t know its biological reactions," Carter gently tapped the sample. "Our job is to make sure our field agents don’t dissolve while wearing it."

Connor’s gaze remained fixed on the material, his fingers unconsciously tracing its edges with a kind of unintentional reverence.

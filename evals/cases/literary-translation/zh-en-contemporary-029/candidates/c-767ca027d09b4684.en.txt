The doctor actually enjoys food.

Basic carbohydrates, salt, glutamate broken down from fats and proteins—all of these keep its mind clear and functioning, providing the energy needed for a qualified doctor throughout the day.

It has a sense of taste, albeit delayed and fleeting, but it rightfully chooses more delicious foods, just like that bowl of bean soup once before.

When the doctor brings the wooden spoon holding the hot soup into the shadows, the warm and thick liquid flows down the throat, and the grainy texture of the bean fibers can still be clearly felt.

The bean paste and melted fat form a lubricating oily film, protecting the mucous membranes from the slight scratching caused by the chopped but still somewhat coarse turnip fibers. When this warmth sinks into the stomach, the rich taste that surges back up arrives slowly. The seasoning is delicate, seemingly the opposite of the bean soup’s miserable appearance—the dense sweetness of the beans combined with the savory flavor of meat, accented by the sweetness of caramelized onions, while the fresh sweetness of the turnip is highlighted by other spices.

For a doctor who has traveled widely but has always been rushing toward a great goal, this is an extremely rare delicacy.

* * *

But—what about that child? What happened to him in the end? The child who, among those afflicted by the plague that it treated, was one of the few from whom it could not detect the plague’s aura?

The doctor always felt that the child was still alive.

It sneered at this thought of its own. Although the child was healthy, without the foul stench of the plague, replaced instead by a reassuring scent of citrus and faint lavender, how could that be? Human lifespans are so short; even a healthy body could not have survived from that era until now.

Such fragile life—also beneficial to it, as it could use its touch to create healing opportunities for itself.

It thought of those poor patients, those pitiful people whose bodies were filled with the foul stench of the plague without any awareness. Even the day before, that patient’s family, despite their modest means, had been hosting it.

They were eating exactly these three things.

These three foods had filled almost all of the doctor’s past time. Since that was the case, what was the point of further distinguishing and waiting for those complex and fleeting taste signals now? Biscuits, salted pork, hard cheese—the flavors of these foods were so familiar to it that it no longer needed to anticipate them, nor would it ever be mistaken.

Site-19, this cold and clean place. These foods were just basic, a mutual benefit among colleagues. They wanted to observe its work, and it only requested basic necessities—that was enough.

After all, it did not need to eat, but it enjoyed food.

So the doctor sped up its eating.

* * *

Almost finished.

The doctor paused, struggling to break free from memories and tangled thoughts.

These tastes disappeared too quickly. It couldn’t even manage to hold onto these sensations.

No need.

It told itself.

It reached out and placed the last small piece of cheese into the shadows. A faint salty and sour taste arose in its senses, then quickly receded like a retreating tide.

The doctor sat quietly like this, letting that thin lingering flavor completely dissipate in its consciousness, as if it had never existed.

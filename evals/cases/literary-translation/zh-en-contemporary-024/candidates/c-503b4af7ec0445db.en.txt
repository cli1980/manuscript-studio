You are a security guard at the station. It’s still dark, more than an hour before the first train. This is troublesome because you are holding a gun, pointing at a man lying on the tracks with large characters painted on him. The man is skinny and bony, reeking of alcohol, but you think he knows exactly what he’s doing. You disengage the safety on your pistol, but he doesn’t move an inch. He is determined to lie there and wait for the train to run him over. That’s exactly why he came here. He drinks to steel his nerves.

“Come on, buddy. I know what you’re here to do,” you shout at him. “I don’t have time to waste with you, and I’m not going to shoot you in the head because that’s what you want. Time’s tight, so I’m going to shoot your leg, and while you’re cursing in pain, I’ll drag you away. So get up! Did you fucking hear me?”

The man doesn’t say anything.

“Are you deaf? Then you can read what this is, right? This is a gun. If you don’t get up, I’m going to shoot your leg.” You kick his calf hard. “How about that? Got it?”

The man rolls his eyes at you but still lies on the tracks, staring at the dim light in the east that’s beginning to brighten. He’s betting you won’t dare pull the trigger — the same old thinking. He doesn’t know that the railway department’s regulations have long since changed, and with ‘Cancer’ backing you up, many violent enforcement actions are now ethically permitted. Of course, many people still can’t bring themselves to do this because it requires a certain psychological toughness. But today, he’s out of luck, because you’re not that kind of person.

You pull the trigger. The bullet pierces his calf, spraying a bright red burst of blood. He lets out a pig-like scream that hurts your eardrums. You spit on the ground, and while he’s yelling and struggling, you grab his shoulder and drag him to the side of the tracks. As expected, he’s as light as a sack half-filled with sand. You actually didn’t need to shoot to subdue him. But no one cares about that detail.

Ignoring his miserable screams, you press the button on your pager and report the situation to the stationmaster.

“I’m good here. The guy’s as weak as a chicken. I need backup.”

“Got it. Xiao Wang’s situation is handled too. He’s coming over now.”

He’s not the only one who comes here seeking to be run over, not even the only one today. There are always people who want to find relief here — those cowardly souls who want to die but can’t bring themselves to do it. You despise such people intensely. They’re the scum of society, neither strong enough to live nor brave enough to die on their own. If you didn’t know that this wouldn’t kill them, you’d really want to shoot these pieces of trash in the head. Damn it, why don’t they just hang themselves in their rooms, or burn charcoal, or drink paraquat? They never think about how many people’s travel plans they disrupt by lying on the tracks. Maybe they don’t have enough brain cells left to consider these things, you think to yourself.

You look down at the man, who is having his last drunken fit. But to your surprise, he doesn’t do anything to you. He waves his hands around, shouting incoherent words. He cries, twitches, and gulps down saliva like a three-and-a-half-year-old child whose favorite toy has been taken away.

Actually, this situation could have been easily avoided. You vaguely remember that last year there was news about building sound barriers around the railway. They didn’t even have to be soundproof walls — just taller than a person would do, anything. But a year has passed, and nothing has happened. So people like you have to get up early and work late every day to chase away the scum of humanity on this suburban railway. You don’t know why it’s like this, but honestly, you don’t really care.

You quietly wait for your partner. You don’t want to spend another second with this man.

The entrance of the curtain shimmered with layers of pale blue light emitted by Cherenkov radiation. Hundreds of stars, shining like pearls, hung upside down in the fleeting sky; they rose for a moment and quickly fell, their brilliance dissolving into the unfathomable abyss of eternal darkness, where countless deeper chaos and nothingness swirled.

Once again, I heard the cold wind of the Siberian plateau howling in my ears, and once again, I smelled the salty, fishy scent of blood. The sensation of weightlessness blurred and confused my consciousness as I dreamily moved along the only path, with deities singing on either side, the entire graveyard of the universe singing, until I gently walked into that good night.

I floated up from the stars, maintaining a position with my hands on the ground to avoid falling into the vomit before me. I swallowed hard, spitting out the burning sensation left in my throat, then stood up.

As far as I could see, there were collapsed shelves and rising flames, thick smoke rushing toward me. I held my breath, bent down, and shielded my face with my arms as I crawled in. The sparkling shards of glass on the ground came from the collapsed bookshelves. "The Story of Fire" and "The Moth Who Chased the Sun" were burning fiercely, performing their final act in this way.

LG sat sideways on a classical wooden chair, turning his head, emitting a burnt smell from the high temperature. I rushed forward, intending to grab his collar and drag him out, but his clothes and skin had melted into the chair like butter. I had no choice but to bear his limp body against my shoulder; he laughed with a strange, joyful voice. His fingers dug into my back with excitement, a strength not typical of a dying person.

The fire spread along the walls and ceiling, greedily consuming everything, distorting the light, making it look like a magnificent stage play coming to an end from a distance. I kicked open the side door and rushed out, but the crew members, who usually loved to gather, were nowhere to be found. Perhaps everyone was dead.

I leaned LG against the wall. Having inhaled a large amount of carbon monoxide, his body was bright red, with blood seeping from his thighs and arms—wounds torn from the skin during the struggle. But these superficial injuries were trivial because his time was running out.

"You’re going to die," I asked bluntly. "Did you get the script? Where's my brother?"

He widened his eyes and leaned closer, so close that I could see the ash-like wrinkles at the corners of his eyes and the remaining eyelashes on his eyelids. LG opened his mouth, but all he managed to do was spit soot in my face.

"Explain, quickly."

His chest heaved violently, a sense of urgency forcing him to grip me tightly, leaning into my ear to say: "If a gun appears in the play, it must fire a bullet in some scene."

Then he let out a long breath and slumped back, collapsing like a deflated balloon.

LG shouldn’t be dying; his life had never ceased since the construction of the Dionysus Theater. Unless a script demanded his death, like a gun, like a bullet, like a dead man.

Standing up, I looked down at LG's corpse. As I said, death is neither glorious nor dignified.

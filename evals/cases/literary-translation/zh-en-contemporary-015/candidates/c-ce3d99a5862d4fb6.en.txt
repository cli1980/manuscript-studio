He has felt as if a part of his brain had been missing since his parents died.

It was the fall of 1949. The police said that the accident resulted from a gas leak. The neighbour said that all three members of his family should've died in the house, yet he was the only one who miraculously survived. He couldn't remember anything. Doctors said it was traumatic amnesia. His brain decided to forget its most painful memory. He had no choice but to believe it, as he really couldn't remember anything. The only thing he remembered was that he lost his parents on that autumn night.

However, it wasn't long before his body started to recall what he had forgotten.

Prolonged headache, accompanied by severe nausea and discomfort, and even occasional vomiting. Panic attacks — a sudden, intense feeling of fear and discomfort — striking at any ordinary moment made it impossible for him to breathe normally. It was like his body forgot how to carry out the process of breathing. What tormented him most were the fragments of memory that seemed to still exist in his mind but simply wouldn't come together to form a complete picture — he couldn't stop trying to recall a past he had never actually remembered, repeatedly piecing together a jigsaw puzzle with most of the pieces missing. He couldn't bring himself to stop, nor would he ever succeed.

Doctors said it was post-traumatic stress disorder, and later said it was a dissociative disorder. Again and again, he tried all sorts of ways to combat his madness, from electroshock to insulin coma. Again and again, he tried, and he failed.

His situation got worse a few years later. He kept talking to himself and wandering in corridors. He couldn't sleep all night, spending a long time staring at the ceiling. His fingers left bloody scratches on walls and his own body. Whenever the nurse tried to put a restraint on him, he would scream so loudly that the whole hospital building could hear him.

In the end, the attending physician had a talk with him.

"There's a kind of surgery," the physician said, "a very effective one. You'll be less painful after having it."

He scanned the consent form wordlessly.

"You need a family member to sign it."

"I don't have any family."

The physician remained silent for a while, pushing the consent form toward him. "You can sign it yourself."

He looked at the form, took up a pen, and signed his name.

He was wheeled into the the operating room. The anesthesiologist asked him to count. He counted to three.

He saw a blinding white light. It was the ceiling lamp, yet it also resembled something from that night. Caught between the effects of the drugs and the ice pick, his brain made one last attempt to recall, but he still couldn't remember anything.

It was already several days later when he woke up.

The sky outside the window was blue and the sunshine was lovely. Someone helped him sit up and asked him how he was feeling.

He turned around and looked at the man in white. His eyes were no longer bloodshot as before; instead, they were clear and bright, like those of a newborn baby.

"I'm fine." He said.

He was fine, indeed. He would no longer suffer from losing his memories or parents, and would no longer have nightmares. Of course, he would neither know where he was, nor know that he had had parents.

He looked out of the window and felt that the world had always been so beautiful.

He was fine.

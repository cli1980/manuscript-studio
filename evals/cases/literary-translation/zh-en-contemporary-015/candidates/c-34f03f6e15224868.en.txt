After his parents died, he always felt as if a piece of his brain had been hollowed out.

It was the autumn of 1949. The police said the accident was caused by a gas leak. Neighbors said that all three members of his family should have died inside the house, and only he miraculously survived. He himself remembered nothing. The doctors said he had traumatic amnesia; his brain chose to forget the most painful memories. He could only believe it because he truly remembered nothing. All he knew was that he lost his parents that autumn night.

But soon, his body began to revisit those things he had forgotten.

Long-lasting headaches accompanied by severe nausea and discomfort, even occasional vomiting. Panic attacks—sudden intense fear and discomfort that made it impossible for him to breathe normally at some ordinary moment, as if his body suddenly forgot how the breathing process should work. The most tormenting was the fragments of memories that seemed to still exist in his brain but could not be pieced together into a picture—he couldn’t stop trying to recall those pasts he never remembered, repeatedly piecing together a puzzle missing most of its parts. He couldn’t control himself to stop, nor could he succeed.

The doctors said it was post-traumatic stress disorder, later said it was dissociative disorder. He tried various desperate methods to fight the madness, from electroconvulsive therapy to insulin coma. Time after time, attempts and failures.

After a few years, his condition worsened. He kept talking to himself, pacing back and forth in the hallway, unable to sleep all night, staring at the ceiling for long periods. His fingers scratched bloody marks on the walls and his own body. When nurses tried to put him in restraints, he screamed so loudly the whole hospital building could hear.

Finally, the attending doctor talked to him once.

“There is a kind of surgery,” the doctor said, “a very effective surgery. After it, you will no longer suffer so much.”

His eyes scanned the consent form but he said nothing.

“You need a family member’s signature.”

“I have no family.”

The doctor was silent for a moment, then pushed the consent form toward him: “You can sign it yourself.”

He looked at the paper, picked up the pen, and signed his name.

He was taken into the operating room. The anesthesiologist told him to count. He counted to three.

He saw a dazzling white light, the lamp on the ceiling, or something like that night. His brain, caught between drugs and the ice pick, made one last attempt to recall, but he still remembered nothing.

When he woke up, it was already several days later.

The sky outside the window was very blue, the sunlight was good. Someone helped him sit up and asked how he felt now.

He turned his head and looked at the person in white. His eyes were no longer bloodshot like before, but clean and clear like a newborn baby’s.

“I’m fine,” he said.

He really was fine. No longer would he suffer from lost memories or parents, no longer have nightmares. Of course, he didn’t know where he was now. Nor would he ever know he once had parents.

He looked out the window and felt the world had always been so beautiful.

He was fine.

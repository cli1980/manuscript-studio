Ever since I came to the big city, I've felt uneasy. Every day is spent meticulously budgeting rent, utilities, food, and daily expenses. Back home, I never felt this strapped even on three thousand a month, yet my parents are still bragging to relatives and friends that I make over ten thousand a month. That’s true, but I can’t save a single cent. Flipping through my ledger, I always feel like I’ve wasted a lot of money, but if I go over it again, I really couldn’t survive without those little guilty pleasures. I’m probably a coward. I think of Rickshaw Boy—how he was so miserable, yet still went to the White House and bought cigarettes. I used to laugh at him when I was a kid.

Today I was dragged into cleaning up someone else’s mess by my boss again. By the time I left the office building and looked up, I could see the moon. But the streetlights hadn’t gone out, and they never will, not even during the day. This city doesn’t allow sleep, but tonight was especially over the top. All the billboards had been taken over by some rat poison advertisement. Such a huge dead rat, paired with that garish bright red background—RGB set to 255, 0, 0, probably—shameless. I trudged toward the subway entrance, still wearing that heavy red uniform, because the boss said we couldn’t take it off even after work. That’s what happens when a psychopath is in charge.

The escalator at the subway entrance was shut off due to power rationing. I walked down step by step. Was the air conditioning off today too because of the power cuts? Why was the empty subway station so hot? I suspected it was just my own agitation, because the station had also been taken over by the rat poison ads. Bright red and dead rats covered the walls and ticket gates. I got on the train, and outside the windows it was all bright red, too.

Money talks, and that’s just how it is.

Today, another overtime shift. Everyone on the street was looking up—turns out there was a once-in-a-century red moon tonight. But what did that have to do with me? Those people were probably local trust-fund kids, able to afford such leisure. The buildings along the street were still plastered with bright red and dead rats, even more brazen than before. Between shop doors and windows, the ads were everywhere, pervasive, to the point that the red glow dyed the streetlights red, spilling over the dead rats scattered across the ground.

The subway station was sweltering, making me anxious—I thought I was having an internal heat flare-up. Now the ads weren’t satisfied with just a few signs; they’d boldly invaded the floor. I don’t know how they did it, but they still glowed bright red. Their background was no longer just solid red—it was printed with neat, tightly packed large characters that read:

Kids love it, family at ease—just use Yisaoguang® Powerful Rodenticide!

The characters stretched into the distance, taking on a striped pattern, with dead rats layered on top. I kept feeling like I was stepping on something, even touching things on the handrails, so I avoided the dead rats, staggering downward in a drunken, crooked gait. The seats and grab handles on the subway were all dead rats too, so I had no choice but to stand with my legs apart like a model striking a pose. The train suddenly accelerated, throwing me to the floor, and immediately I heard a dense chorus of squelches, crunches, and… human screams.

That night, I showered for over an hour, and nearly ended up late for work the next day.
